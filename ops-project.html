Your Team Runs Real Infrastructure

The first real work of the year — Wk 1–2 of the Fall Term.

Before we start pitching and building your own projects, three teams are going to stand up and run three pieces of real, open-source software that the whole class will actually use — not a simulation of production software, the real thing.

The three systems

TeamOwnsWhat it does
ARocket.ChatOur class's actual chat platform, for the whole year
BPlaneWhere the ops teams (not your capstone teams) track the work of keeping these three systems alive
CUptime KumaA public status page showing, in real time, whether Rocket.Chat and the tracker are actually up

All three run on one always-on Mac Mini on the school network. This isn't a cloud project — everything here is reachable over the school Wi-Fi, nothing needs a Google Cloud account.

Why this comes first

Every other assignment this year is eventually forgiving — if your own project breaks in the spring, that's material for a retrospective. If the class chat server breaks, people can't talk to each other today. That's not a metaphor. It's the most honest way we know to teach you what "production" actually means before you scope your own idea.

What "your job" actually means

Your team doesn't just get this running once — you own it for the year. That includes:

  • Getting it deployed and working in the first place.
  • Keeping it running: if it goes down, you're the ones who fix it, not Mr. Meyer.
  • The Mac Mini has FileVault disk encryption on, on purpose. If it reboots, someone needs to be physically present with the disk password to unlock it before anything comes back — that's a real, intended constraint, not an oversight. Your team should know how to handle it.
  • You'll have SSH access to the machine to check on and restart your service remotely — Mr. Meyer will set this up with you directly.
  • When something breaks, you write a short incident report: what broke, why, what you did about it. Same structure as a sprint retro — Observation, Root cause, Experiment.

Team B's tool: Plane

Mr. Meyer picked Plane directly, mainly because it has solid built-in support for Google sign-in, which matters for onboarding the whole class without another password to manage. Your job is to actually stand it up and get it working — including Google SSO, once the underlying network piece that makes SSO possible is sorted out.

The actual finish line

This project isn't done when your team's service technically runs — it's done when all three systems are up and everyone can see that they're up, in one place, on Team C's status page. That last step takes all three teams working together, on purpose:

  • Team A and Team C: Team C needs Rocket.Chat's address to add it as a monitor — point it at http://<the-mac's-address>:3000/api/info, a real endpoint people use for exactly this.
  • Team B and Team C: same idea for Plane — point the monitor at its login page, or better, go look together at whatever health check is already defined in Plane's own docker-compose file (worth doing anyway — it's basically your reflection question about walking through that file, just done early).
  • Team C: set up a real notification (email is simplest) so someone actually gets pinged when a service goes down, instead of relying on someone happening to glance at the status page.

None of you are individually "done" until all three show green together. That's the actual point — not three teams each quietly confident their own thing works, but one system the whole class can trust.

What you're actually here to learn

This project is testing something different from your capstone project, on purpose:

  1. Reading and operating a real codebase you didn't write. A real install guide, real configuration, a real database dependency — and being able to say specifically how that's different from two years of CS and Data Structures & Algorithms.
  2. Owning reliability, not just getting something to work once. "It runs" and "it stays running" are different jobs.
  3. Evaluating and choosing real tools under real constraints (Team B especially).
  4. Configuring, not just coding. Auth, environment variables, containers, backups — the invisible mass of a real deployment that never shows up when an assignment just needs to "run on localhost."
  5. Communicating clearly when it actually matters. Your classmates are the real, affected audience for your incident reports — not just a grade.

AI use on this project

This project's AI policy is deliberately different from how you'll use AI on your own capstone build, because the risk here is different. On your own project, the worry is "AI wrote code you don't understand." Here, you're barely writing original code — you're deploying and configuring someone else's real software — so the actual trap is subtler: asking AI to explain an error and pasting in the fix without understanding why it worked.

Use AI freely, out in the open, for

Explaining unfamiliar code or config, translating a cryptic error message, summarizing documentation, drafting first-pass notes for Team B's comparison. This is how working engineers actually use these tools — no reason to pretend otherwise.

Use AI to help, but explain what you shipped

Any AI-suggested change to a docker-compose file or config should be understood line by line before you apply it. If you can't explain why a line is there, don't ship it yet.

No AI, by design

Your incident writeups need to be in your own words. These exist specifically to check whether you actually understand what happened, and an AI-smoothed writeup defeats the point. Expect a short "walk me through it" conversation on at least one incident per team, per term.

Reflection

Once all three systems are live, the whole class does a short in-class writing assignment on what this project taught you. Mr. Meyer will walk you through it when the time comes — nothing to prepare in advance.
